Shelter and staying at a safe temperature

Shelter is not about comfort. It is about keeping your body temperature between roughly 36 and 38 °C while the weather does its worst. In a house, that means choosing one room and sealing it. Outside, it means four walls, a roof and something dry underneath you.

Rule one: stay put if you can

In a storm, a flood, a heat wave or a power cut, your own home is almost always safer than a car, a field or an improvised camp. Travelling in bad conditions is the single most common way people die in disasters. Shelter in place unless the building itself is unsafe (flood water rising, structural damage, fire, gas smell).

Cold: warming one room

  1. Stop the draughts. Rolled towels at door bottoms, blankets over windows, tape over obvious gaps. One sealed room beats a whole cold house.
  2. Insulate the floor and the ceiling of your space. Rugs, cardboard, blankets, mattresses. Heat leaves through cold surfaces.
  3. Pick a small room and live in it. Close the doors to the rest of the house and hang a blanket across the doorway.
  4. Warm people, not air. Hot water bottles, blankets, dry clothes, warm food and drink, and sharing body heat. Never use a barbecue, gas heater or generator indoors.
  5. Sleep off the floor on a mattress, sofa cushions or a camp bed, and wear a hat. A third of heat loss at night is upward from an uninsulated body.
  6. Move every hour. Ten minutes of gentle exercise — walking, arm swings, tidying — raises core temperature far more than sitting still.

Never bring a charcoal barbecue, camping stove, patio heater or generator indoors, into a garage, or into a tent — even with a window open. Carbon monoxide kills within minutes at high concentrations and gives no warning.

Heat: the opposite problem, equally deadly

  1. Close windows, curtains and blinds on the sunny side during the day; open everything at night when the air outside is cooler than inside.
  2. Drink before you are thirsty. Add a pinch of salt to your water if you are sweating heavily and eating little.
  3. Wet a towel or T-shirt and drape it over your neck and shoulders. Evaporation cools you far faster than a fan alone in dry air.
  4. Do heavy work at dawn. Rest in the hottest hours.
  5. Check on anyone elderly, alone, or on medication — they feel heat less and dehydrate faster.

Heat stroke is an emergency: hot dry skin, confusion, collapse, temperature above 40 °C. Cool the person immediately with water, wet cloths and moving air, and get medical help.

If you must spend a night outdoors

  1. Site: high ground, out of the wind, not under a dead branch, not in a dry riverbed (flash floods come from rain you cannot see), not on an animal trail.
  2. Insulate underneath. A 10 cm layer of dry leaves, bracken or pine needles is worth more than a blanket over the top. You lose heat downwards faster than upwards.
  3. Build small. A debris hut, a snow trench, or a lean-to of branches woven with leaves, sized to your body — a shelter you can just fit into warms up; a big one stays cold.
  4. Waterproof the top with bark, a tarp, a poncho or a thick layer of leaves. Slope it so rain runs away from you, not into you.
  5. Mark your position. Three of anything (whistles, fires in a triangle, bright cloth) is the universal distress signal. Whistle blasts carry much further than shouting and cost almost no energy.

Water in the shelter

Never let meltwater, drips or condensation soak your bedding. Put a waterproof sheet between you and the ground and keep a dry set of clothes in a sealed bag that you only wear for sleeping. Dry clothes are a survival item, not a luxury.

Home hardening, before you need it

  • One cupboard with: torches, spare batteries, a battery or wind-up radio, first-aid kit, water, a fire extinguisher, a whistle, copies of documents, cash in small notes, and a printed list of phone numbers.
  • One bag per person by the door: three days of clothes, water, snacks, medicines, a photocopy of ID, a power bank, and something to keep children calm.
  • Know two ways out of every room you sleep in, and where the water, gas and electricity shut-offs are. Label them. Practise once a year, in the dark.
  • Keep the car half full and a blanket, torch and boots in the boot. A car is a good shelter in a snowstorm and a terrible one in a flood.
  • Write things down. Phone numbers, medical conditions, allergy lists, and the location of your documents — on paper. A dead phone holds nothing.

The 20-minute test: turn off the power and water at the mains one evening, and see how the household copes for an hour. Whatever you had to go looking for, in the dark, is what belongs in the cupboard.
