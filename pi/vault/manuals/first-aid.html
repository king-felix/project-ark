First aid: the first ten minutes

This page covers only what a bystander can do before help arrives: bleeding, breathing, choking, burns, breaks and shock. It is not a medical qualification. If a phone works, call emergency services first, then act.

Call for help before anything else if you can. Put the phone on speaker so you can keep working while you talk. In most countries the emergency number works even from a phone with no SIM, and from a locked phone.

The order of priorities

  1. Is the scene safe? Traffic, fire, electricity, water, gas, falling debris. You cannot help anyone if you become the second casualty.
  2. Is the person responding? Shout, tap the shoulders. No response means check breathing.
  3. Is the person breathing? Look at the chest, listen at the mouth, feel for breath for 10 seconds.
  4. Stop major bleeding. Catastrophic bleeding kills in three minutes.
  5. Treat shock and keep them warm while help comes.

No breathing, no pulse: CPR

  1. Lay the person on their back on a firm surface.
  2. Push hard and fast in the centre of the chest: 100–120 compressions per minute, about 5–6 cm deep, letting the chest come all the way back up. The beat of "Stayin' Alive" is the right speed.
  3. If you are trained and willing, give 2 rescue breaths after every 30 compressions. If not, keep doing compressions only — hands-only CPR saves lives.
  4. Keep going, swapping with someone else every 2 minutes, until help arrives, the person starts breathing, or you are physically unable.

For a child, use one hand (or two fingers for a baby) and press about one third of the chest depth. For anyone found in water, get them out first, then start with 5 rescue breaths.

Choking (adult)

  • Can they cough? Encourage coughing. Do not slap their back yet.
  • Cannot cough, speak or breathe: 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand, leaning them forward. Then 5 abdominal thrusts (hands clasped above the navel, pull sharply in and up). Alternate 5 and 5 until the object comes out.
  • If they become unresponsive, lower them to the floor and start CPR.
  • Anyone who received abdominal thrusts must be seen by a doctor afterwards.

Severe bleeding

  1. Press directly and hard on the wound with the cleanest cloth you have. Use both hands, put your body weight into it.
  2. Do not lift the cloth to look. Add more cloth on top if it soaks through.
  3. Once bleeding slows, bandage firmly over the cloth. Check fingers or toes stay warm and pink — if they turn white or blue, loosen slightly.
  4. For an arm or leg wound that will not stop, and if you have been trained, use a tourniquet 5–7 cm above the wound (never over a joint) and write the time on the person's forehead or arm. Do not loosen it.
  5. Lay the person down, raise the injured limb if you can, and keep them warm — bleeding causes shock quickly.

Burns

  1. Cool it with cool running water for 20 minutes. Not ice, not butter, not toothpaste, not a frozen steak. Twenty minutes, and start it as soon as you can — it keeps working for up to three hours after the burn.
  2. Remove rings, watches and tight clothing near the burn before it swells.
  3. Cover loosely with cling film (laid, not wrapped) or a clean, non-fluffy cloth.
  4. Do not burst blisters. Do not peel clothing stuck to a burn.
  5. Give pain relief if you have it, and keep the person warm — cooling a large burn can chill the whole body.

Go to hospital for: any burn bigger than the person's palm, burns on the face, hands, feet, genitals or across a joint, all electrical and chemical burns, any burn that is white, brown or charred with no pain, and any burn in a baby, an elderly person or someone with a chronic illness.

Breaks, sprains and head injuries

  • Suspected broken bone: do not try to straighten it. Support it in the position found, using a splint padded with cloth and bound above and below the break. Check fingers and toes stay warm and pink.
  • Sprain: rest, ice (10 minutes at a time, wrapped in cloth — never direct on skin), compression, elevation. If you cannot bear weight, treat it as a break.
  • Head injury: any loss of consciousness, vomiting, worsening headache, unequal pupils, confusion, clear fluid from the nose or ears, or drowsiness means urgent medical help. Keep the person still and awake.
  • Suspected neck or back injury: do not move them unless they are in immediate danger. Support the head in line with the spine.

Shock

Shock is not psychological — it is the circulation failing. Signs: pale, cold, clammy skin, fast weak pulse, rapid shallow breathing, thirst, anxiety, confusion.

  1. Lay the person down and raise their legs about 30 cm, unless you suspect a spinal, pelvic or leg injury or they are struggling to breathe.
  2. Keep them warm with blankets or coats — but do not overheat.
  3. Do not give food or drink; moisten the lips with water if they are thirsty.
  4. Reassure them, stay with them, and keep checking breathing.

Allergic reaction

Swelling of the lips or tongue, a tight throat, widespread hives, wheeze, vomiting or collapse after a sting, food or medicine is anaphylaxis. Use the person's adrenaline auto-injector immediately (outer thigh, through clothing is fine if necessary), call for help, lay them flat with legs raised, and be ready to start CPR. A second dose can be given after 5–15 minutes if there is no improvement.

ItemWhy
Sterile gauze pads, 5 × 5 and 10 × 10 cmWound pressure and dressing

Painkillers, rehydration sachetsPain, dehydration

Check the kit twice a year: replace anything expired, anything opened, and anything you borrowed and forgot to put back.

This page summarises widely taught first-aid practice; it does not replace training. A one-day course is the best hour-per-year investment you can make.
