Food: store it, keep it safe, make it last

Food is rarely the first thing you run out of in an emergency — but bad food can put a whole household on its back in a day. Two rules cover most of it: keep it dry, keep it cool, and when in doubt, throw it out.

Two weeks in a cupboard

Buy what you already eat and rotate it. A store cupboard that is unfamiliar is a cupboard you will not use.

Add salt, sugar and oil to the list before anything else: they make dull staples edible, and they are what most emergency stores lack.

Storing it so it survives

  • Cool, dark, dry, off the floor. Heat and light destroy vitamins and fats; damp grows mould; the floor invites rodents.
  • Decant into sealed containers — glass jars, food-grade buckets with lids, or thick plastic boxes. Label with contents and date.
  • Freeze flour, rice and dried fruit for 48 hours before long storage to kill weevils and their eggs, then store sealed.
  • Rotate. New purchases go to the back; the oldest comes forward. Write the date on everything in marker you can read in the dark.
  • Keep a can opener with the cans. Two. This is the most forgotten item in every emergency store.

Never eat: cans that are bulging, leaking or smell wrong when opened; anything from a jar with a lifted lid or a mouldy surface; food that has been above 5 °C for more than two hours (one hour above 30 °C); anything that tastes or smells "off", even slightly.

When the fridge and freezer stop

  1. Keep the doors closed. A full freezer holds temperature for about 48 hours; a half-full one for 24. A fridge keeps food cold for about 4 hours.
  2. Eat the fridge first, then the freezer, then the cupboard. Cook in the order things spoil.
  3. Ice crystals and firm, cold food that is still below 5 °C can be refrozen or cooked. Food that has thawed to room temperature should be cooked and eaten now, not refrozen.
  4. Fill the freed space with bottles of water — they hold the cold and give you drinking water later.

Sterilise jars by washing, then heating in an oven at 120 °C for 15 minutes (or boiling for 10), and fill while both jar and contents are hot. A "pop" as the lid seals is the sign to look for.

Foraging: safe rules only

  • Eat only what you can identify with certainty. "Probably" is not good enough. Learn ten safe plants well rather than a hundred badly.
  • Never eat a plant you have not seen identified by someone knowledgeable, or a reliable guide, in the same region.
  • Avoid anything growing by a road, on sprayed land, near a factory, or downstream of a sewer.
  • Skip white gilled mushrooms, anything with a ring or cup at the base, and any berry you cannot name. No mushroom is worth the risk.
  • Introduce any new wild food in a tiny amount, and not at all to children, pregnant women or anyone unwell.

Suspect poisoning? Do not induce vomiting. Keep the plant or packaging for identification, rinse the mouth, and get medical help immediately. Store household chemicals and medicines apart from food — most accidental poisonings are a mix-up, not a mystery.

Cooking with very little

  1. Soak before you cook. Beans and lentils soaked overnight cook in half the fuel.
  2. Use a lid and a lid-weight. A covered pot boils noticeably faster than an open one.
  3. Insulate the pot ("haybox" cooking): bring rice or stew to a boil, then wrap the lidded pot in blankets, coats or straw and leave it for 2–3 hours. It keeps cooking with no fuel at all.
  4. Cook once, eat twice. One big pot in the morning covers the evening meal.
  5. Save the water from cooking vegetables for soup — it carries vitamins and flavour.

Keeping the household fed and calm

Eat on a schedule, even if the meals are plain. Meals at normal times are the fastest way to reduce fear in children. Keep one treat item — chocolate, biscuits, coffee — for morale. Share responsibilities: someone cooks, someone manages water, someone manages the toilet and rubbish. Written on a card and stuck to the wall, a rota removes most of the arguments.
