Power, light, radios and batteries

No electricity means no pumping, no heating, no phone, no news. You do not need to run the house — you need light, information, a charged phone and one way to recharge a small battery. That is a much smaller problem.

The list, in order of importance

  1. Light. LED lanterns and head torches, plus spare batteries. Light prevents falls, which are the most common injury in a blackout.
  2. Information. A battery or wind-up radio, or a phone with an offline map and a downloaded emergency guide. Radio still works when mobile networks collapse; it tells you where the water points and shelters are.
  3. Phone power. A power bank, kept charged, plus a small solar panel. A phone is a torch, a camera, a map, a store of documents and a way to reach people.
  4. Heat and cooking. See the Fire manual. Gas cylinders, if legal and ventilated, beat electric hobs that have no power.
  5. Medical equipment. If someone depends on powered equipment, that is the top of the list — register it with your utility and the local emergency service before an outage.

Rough maths that keeps you honest: watts × hours = watt-hours. A 100 Wh power station will charge a phone eight times, run a lantern for two nights, or boil a kettle for three minutes.

Batteries without mystery

  • Store them outside the device, in a dry bag, at room temperature. Cold halves capacity; heat kills it permanently.
  • Alkaline cells leak if left for years — a leaked cell destroys a torch. Check every six months, replace anything with crust on the ends, and never mix old and new cells in one device.
  • Lithium AA cells cost more and last far longer, work down to −20 °C, and do not leak. Worth it for anything you store for emergencies.
  • Rechargeable (NiMH) cells lose about 1 % a month; recharge them every few months. They are cheaper over years if you actually cycle them.
  • Lithium-ion packs (phones, banks, tools): keep them between 40 % and 80 % for storage, never charge below 0 °C, never puncture or crush, and stop using any pack that swells.

Solar, realistically

  1. Aim the panel squarely at the sun — a 30° error can cost a third of the output. Re-aim it three times a day, or mount it on a south-facing (north-facing in the southern hemisphere) slope.
  2. Keep the glass clean and out of shade. A single leaf across a panel can halve its output.
  3. Expect 20–40 % of the rated wattage in real use: a "20 W" folding panel typically delivers 5–8 W into a phone, on a good day.
  4. Charge phones directly from the panel at midday; use the battery bank as a buffer, not the phone as one.
  5. Cloudy days produce 10–25 % of clear-sky output — plan for two sunny days a week, not seven.

Generators, honestly

  • Position: outdoors, at least 5 metres from doors, windows and vents, and never below a window. Exhaust gas is invisible and odourless.
  • Never refuel a running generator, and let it cool before moving it.
  • Never back-feed a house by plugging a generator into a wall socket. That can kill a utility worker and destroy your wiring. Use a proper changeover switch, fitted by an electrician.
  • Load small: a 2 kW generator will run a fridge, a few lamps and a router, or a heater — not both. Add loads one at a time and listen for the engine labouring.
  • Store fuel outside, in a proper container, away from heat, and use it within a few months.

Car batteries and jump leads (emergency only)

A car battery holds a lot of energy and can deliver enough current to weld metal. If you use one to charge a phone through a 12 V adapter, keep it upright, ventilated, and never short the terminals with a tool or jewellery. Do not attempt to charge a lead-acid battery indoors if it is gassing — a spark plus hydrogen is an explosion.

Never connect a car battery or generator directly to household wiring, and never use a car battery to run mains equipment through a cheap inverter while it is wet or on a metal surface.

Radio: your link to the outside

  • A wind-up or battery AM/FM radio is the most reliable news source in a long outage, and local stations broadcast emergency information continuously.
  • Note the frequencies of your national emergency station and any local station that carries official announcements — write them on a card taped to the radio.
  • Listen at fixed times (for example every hour on the hour) rather than leaving it on, to save batteries.
  • A short piece of wire clipped to the antenna greatly improves reception in a basement or a valley.

The 10-minute power audit

  1. Fill one box with: head torch, lantern, radio, power bank, spare batteries (AA and AAA), a 12 V car charger, and a small solar panel.
  2. Charge everything on the first of every month, whether or not you used it.
  3. Write the recharge date on a label on the box. A power bank at 30 % for six months is a power bank you cannot rely on.
  4. Download offline maps and this library onto every phone and one old phone kept as a spare.
  5. Know where the breaker panel is, and which switch turns off the sockets. Label it in large letters.
