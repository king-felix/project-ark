Fire, warmth and cooking without power

Fire gives heat, light, safe water and cooked food. It also kills people through carbon monoxide and house fires. The order below is deliberate: safety first, then three ways to make a flame, then how to keep it going.

Carbon monoxide has no smell. Never burn charcoal, gas, petrol or a generator indoors, in a garage, in a tent, or in any room with a closed door. If anyone gets a headache, nausea or drowsiness in a heated room, everyone goes outside now and the source is put out.

Before you strike a match

  • Clear a circle of 3 metres of anything that can burn. On grass, scrape to bare soil. Never light a fire on peat or dry roots — they smoulder underground and surface hours later.
  • Keep water, sand or a fire extinguisher within arm's reach. A bucket of water costs nothing and saves houses.
  • Never leave a fire unattended, and put it fully out before sleeping: water, stir, water again, then check the ashes with the back of your hand.
  • Indoors, only use a stove, fireplace or heater designed for indoors, with a working flue. Open a window slightly for ventilation.

Three reliable ways to light a fire

1. Matches and lighters (store them properly)

Keep matches in a sealed bag or screw-top jar with the striker strip. Lighters lose gas when cold — warm one in your hands or armpit for a minute before trying. Store a spare lighter in the car, one in the kitchen, one in the emergency kit; the day you need one is the day you cannot find one.

2. Ferrocerium rod + striker

  1. Prepare a fist-sized bundle of dry tinder: birch bark, dry grass, charred cloth, a cotton ball with a smear of petroleum jelly, or the fluff from a tumble dryer.
  2. Hold the rod steady, press the striker against it at 45°, and pull the striker back fast so sparks land on the tinder.
  3. Cup the tinder, blow gently and steadily from 20 cm until it flames, then feed in pencil-thin twigs.

3. Battery and steel wool (last resort)

Stretch a fine-grade steel-wool pad, touch a 9 V battery terminal to it — the pad glows and ignites. Works wet, works cold, works when everything else is lost. Keep a pad sealed in a plastic bag; it rusts.

4. Sun and a lens

A magnifying glass, spectacle lens, or the bottom of a polished drinks can focuses sunlight into a point. Tinder must be bone dry and dark (charred cloth is ideal). Slow, but it needs no fuel at all.

Laying a fire that actually burns

  1. Tinder: a loose handful of the driest, finest material you can find. Air must move through it.
  2. Kindling: twigs from pencil-thickness up to finger-thickness, dead and snapped off the tree, not picked off the ground.
  3. Fuel: split logs no thicker than your wrist. Green or damp wood smokes and steals heat.

Two shapes work: a teepee (lean kindling against a central bundle — quick flame, good for boiling) and a log cabin (four small logs in a square, tinder inside — steadier, better for cooking and keeping embers). Start small, add gradually, and let the fire breathe.

Wet weather and wind

  • Rain rarely wets the inside of a dead branch: split wet wood and use the dry core.
  • Dry tinder can be carried against your skin inside a jacket; body heat is a real tool.
  • In wind, build a low fire behind a wall of stones or logs, or dig a shallow trench and burn in it. Never build next to a tent or a dry hedge.
  • Birch bark contains oil and burns even when wet. Pine resin, dry pine cones and dry seed heads are near-matches.

Keeping warm without a fire

Most hypothermia is caused by wet and wind, not by extreme cold.

  1. Get out of the wind and off the ground. A layer of cardboard, dry leaves, pine boughs or a foam mat under you stops earth pulling heat out of your body.
  2. Remove wet clothing and put on dry layers, even if that means using the clothes from your bag. Wet cotton is worse than nothing.
  3. Layer up: a wicking base layer, an insulating mid layer (fleece, wool, down), and a windproof/waterproof shell. Loose is warmer than tight — trapped air is the insulation.
  4. Cover the head, neck, hands and feet. Heat loss from an uncovered head is large and cheap to fix.
  5. Eat and drink. Sugar and fat are fuel; warm sweet drinks help. Alcohol widens blood vessels and makes you feel warm while you lose heat — do not use it for warmth.
  6. Share body heat: two people in one sleeping bag with an insulating mat beneath stay far warmer than two in separate bags.

Hypothermia signs: shivering that stops, slurred speech, clumsy hands, confusion, drowsiness. Action: get the person out of the wind, remove wet clothes, wrap in dry layers including the head, give warm sweet drinks if fully conscious, and warm the core (chest, neck, groin) — not the arms and legs. Handle gently. Call for help.

Frostbite signs: skin that is white, waxy, hard and numb. Warm in water at about 37 °C (comfortably warm to your elbow, never hot), for 20–30 minutes, once, and never rub, never use a fire, never re-freeze.

Cooking on a fire

MethodBest forNotes
Embers + flat stonesflatbread, dryingStones from a river can crack or explode when heated — take them from dry ground, or use a pan.
Hanging pot over flamessoup, boiling waterFlames burn the outside of a pot; embers cook better.
Rock ring, coals insideone-pot mealsFeed the fire an hour before you cook, then cook on the coals.
Foil packet in embersvegetables, fish20–30 minutes, turn once, open carefully away from your face.

Cook pork, poultry and fish thoroughly — no pink, juices running clear. Reheat leftovers to steaming hot. If in doubt, cook it longer. Food poisoning in an emergency is far more dangerous than in normal life.

Candles, lamps, batteries — indoors

  • Battery LED lanterns are the safest indoor light. Rechargeable ones plus a small solar panel cover a long outage.
  • Candles: keep them on a metal plate, away from curtains, never in a room where someone might fall asleep, never carried while walking.
  • Store spare batteries outside the device — a leaking cell ruins a torch you were counting on.
  • A head torch keeps both hands free and lights what you are actually looking at. One in every bag.

Putting a fire out properly

  1. Stop feeding it and let it burn down.
  2. Pour water slowly over the whole bed, not just the flames.
  3. Stir the ashes with a stick and pour again.
  4. Hold the back of your hand close to the ashes. If you feel warmth, it is still alive. Repeat.
