Sanitation and hygiene when the water stops

In real disasters, disease kills more people than the event itself — and almost all of it is preventable with soap, separation and a plan for human waste. This is the least glamorous page in the library and the one most worth reading.

The three separations keep a household healthy: drinking water apart from washing water, clean hands apart from dirty hands, and human waste apart from food, water and living space. Everything below serves those three.

Hands: the single most effective action

  1. Wet hands, apply soap, rub all surfaces — backs, between fingers, thumbs, nails — for 20 seconds (sing "Happy Birthday" twice).
  2. Rinse with clean water poured from a jug, not by dipping into a shared bowl.
  3. Dry on a clean cloth or in the air. A damp shared towel spreads germs.
  4. Wash: before food, after the toilet, after changing a nappy, after handling waste or animals, after coming in from outside.

With no running water, a tippy tap works: a 5 litre container with a small hole near the lid, hung from a branch or hook, tipped by a string. A bar of soap on a string beside it. Hands stay clean, water use drops to a cupful per wash.

No soap? Wood ash rubbed on wet hands works as an emergency substitute. Hand sanitiser is a poor second to soap and useless on visibly dirty hands.

Toilets when the mains stop

  1. First flush: a modern toilet can be flushed by pouring 4–6 litres of water straight into the bowl from waist height — it does not need the cistern. Do this only while the sewer is working.
  2. Then stop flushing. Once the sewer is blocked or the water is off, flushing just fills the bowl and backs up the house.
  3. Line the bowl with a double plastic bag. After each use add a scoop of sawdust, ash, dry leaves or soil, and keep the lid down. This controls smell and flies.
  4. Full bags are tied, sealed, and stored well away from food and water until collection is possible.
  5. Long outage: build a twin-pit latrine outdoors, at least 15 metres from any well or stream, downhill of your water source and above the flood line. One pit in use, one resting. Cover the hole with a board and a lid when not in use.
  6. Never put waste into a storm drain, a river, a garden or a pile that rain can reach.

Waste water and rubbish

  • Grey water (washing up, laundry, bathing) can go on non-food plants or into a soak pit — a 60 cm hole filled with gravel, covered with a board.
  • Separate rubbish into: food waste (bury or compost, covered), burnable paper and card, and everything else bagged. Never burn plastic indoors or near food.
  • Keep bins lidded and away from doors. Flies are the main route from waste to food.
  • Wear gloves for any waste handling, and wash hands afterwards even if you did.

Laundry and body washing

  • Wash clothing in the order it will be worn next to the skin, and always rinse thoroughly — detergent left in fabric irritates skin and holds damp.
  • Wash the whole body with a cloth and a bowl of water, in this order: face, hands, armpits, groin, feet. Change socks and underwear daily even if nothing else is clean.
  • Wash babies' and children's bottoms with plain water and a cloth; dry fully. Nappy rash becomes a real problem fast without laundry.
  • Dry everything completely before wearing. Damp clothing in cold weather is a direct route to hypothermia.

Pests, in a hurry

ProblemWhat works
FliesLidded bins, screens on windows, sticky traps. Flies go where waste is.
RodentsEverything stored in sealed containers, no food scraps left out, gaps under doors blocked with wire wool. Traps along walls, not in open floor.
MosquitoesDump standing water within 100 metres, cover water containers, sleep under a net (tucked in).
Bed bugsWash bedding at the hottest the fabric allows and tumble dry hot; bag infested items outside the house.
LiceFine combing on wet hair, every few days, repeated for two weeks.

Wounds in a dirty environment

  1. Clean your hands and put on clean covering before touching a wound.
  2. Irrigate well with clean water — the mechanical washing matters more than the antiseptic. Pour from a height, and use a lot of water.
  3. Remove dirt with clean tweezers. Do not dig deeper than you can see.
  4. Cover with a sterile dressing; change daily, or sooner if it gets wet or dirty.
  5. Watch for infection: spreading redness, warmth, swelling, pus, fever, red streaks. Get medical help — infection is the main way small injuries become serious when pharmacies are shut.

Tetanus and vaccination

Deep or dirty wounds and anything punctured by metal need a tetanus check within 48 hours if you are not up to date. Keep a written record of the household's vaccinations with your documents — you cannot look it up when phones and systems are down.

A 30-minute sanitation upgrade

  1. Put a bucket with a lid and a scoop of ash, soil or sawdust next to the toilet, and a bin with a lid for used bags.
  2. Hang or set up the tippy tap, with soap on a string, between the toilet and the kitchen.
  3. Move all bins away from doors, lids on.
  4. Clear standing water from the garden, buckets and gutters.
  5. Put a paper towel or cloth in every room used for food handling, and agree who empties the bins and when. A job with a name attached gets done.
