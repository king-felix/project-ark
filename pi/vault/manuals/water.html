Water: find it, make it safe, store it

A person can survive roughly three days without water. Nothing else in this library matters until this page does. Read it once now, while the taps still work.

How much you actually need

UsePer person, per day
Drinking3 litres (more in heat, hard work, illness)
Cooking + cleaning2 litres minimum
Washing hands / hygiene2 litres
Planning figure7 litres

Infants, pregnant women, the elderly and anyone sick need more. A dog needs about 1 litre per 10 kg of body weight. Do not ration drinking water to save it — dehydration makes you weak and useless while you still have supplies.

Where to look, in this order

  1. Sealed bottles and cans already in your house, car, shed, or a nearby shop. Untouched factory seals are safe for years.
  2. Your own plumbing. A house's pipes and the water heater hold tens of litres. Open the highest tap first to let air in, then drain from the lowest point. Turn off the mains first so dirty water cannot back-fill.
  3. Toilet cisterns (the tank, not the bowl) hold clean water — provided no chemical cleaner was added.
  4. Rain water off a roof, collected in clean buckets. Skip the first ten minutes of rain (it washes the roof).
  5. Streams, rivers, lakes. Moving water beats still water; still clear water beats muddy. Take it from upstream of any settlement or livestock.
  6. Ground water. Dig at the outside of a bend, below the vegetation line, in low ground. Water appears sooner in sand and gravel than in clay. Let the hole fill, then scoop from the top; it settles overnight.

Never drink these, even in desperation: flood water, water with a fuel or chemical smell, water from radiators or heating systems, swimming-pool water, sea water, and anything you had to scoop out of a toilet bowl. Boiling does not remove fuel, salt or antifreeze — it concentrates them.

Making it safe

Boiling — the one method that always works

  1. Filter out cloudiness first (a clean cloth folded four times, or let it settle and pour off the clear part). Cloudy water boils less effectively and tastes worse.
  2. Bring to a rolling boil — bubbles breaking the surface, not just steam.
  3. Hold it there for 1 minute (3 minutes above 2,000 m).
  4. Cool covered, in the same pot. Do not add ice.

Boiling kills bacteria, viruses and parasites. It does not remove lead, salt or pesticides — if the water tastes chemical, boiling is not the answer.

No stove? Two chemical options

  • Unscented household bleach (5–9 % sodium hypochlorite, no soap, no perfume): 2 drops per litre of clear water, 4 drops if cloudy. Stir, wait 30 minutes, smell it — a faint chlorine smell means it worked. Double the dose for very cold water and wait an hour.
  • Water treatment tablets (chlorine dioxide or iodine): follow the packet, and give it the full contact time. Iodine is not for pregnant women, people with thyroid problems, or for more than a few weeks.

Filters and UV pens

A pump or gravity filter rated to 0.2 microns or finer removes bacteria and parasites but not viruses, and never removes chemicals. UV pens only work on clear water and need a working battery. Treat both as a convenient first step: filter, then boil or chlorinate.

Quick field test for cloudiness: fill a clear bottle and look through it at a page of print. If you can read the print, it is clear enough for the 2-drops bleach dose. If not, use 4 drops and pre-filter.

Storing water before an emergency

  • Two weeks of water for everyone in the house: 7 L × people × 14 days. For four people that is about 400 litres — realistic in a garage or under beds if you use stackable 10–20 L containers.
  • Use food-grade containers with screw lids. Never store drinking water in old milk jugs (they degrade and cannot be cleaned) or in containers that held anything chemical.
  • Fill containers to the top, seal, label with the date, keep out of sunlight and off concrete floors (insulation, not superstition — it keeps the water cooler).
  • Replace treated tap water every 6 months. Replace store-bought sealed water at its printed date.
  • Rotate: drink the oldest first, refill, re-date. Practise this twice a year when the clocks change.

If the taps stop

  1. Fill every pot, kettle, jug and clean bucket immediately, before pressure drops. Toilets and hot-water tanks can be drained later.
  2. Turn off the incoming mains so contaminated water cannot enter your pipes.
  3. Set up one "drinking water" container with a lid and a tap or ladle, and keep all washing water in a separate, clearly marked bucket. Cross-contamination is the usual cause of illness after a disaster.
  4. Wash hands after every toilet visit and before any food handling — 20 seconds, soap, and if there is no running water, pour from a jug.

Signs you are dehydrated

Dark yellow urine, headache, dizziness on standing, dry mouth, no tears, confusion. Drink small amounts often rather than a large amount at once. Add a pinch of salt and a spoon of sugar to a litre of water if someone has been sweating heavily for hours — that is an oral rehydration drink, and it is far better than plain water for heavy fluid loss.
