Everyday fixes around the house

Most household repairs are not clever — they are a matter of using the right fixing, the right glue, and the right amount of force. This guide covers the jobs that keep a home dry, warm, shut and working when nobody is coming to help.

Rule of thumb: if it will hold weight, it must reach something solid — a stud, a joist, brick, or a proper hollow-wall anchor. Everything hung on plasterboard alone eventually falls down, usually at 3 a.m.

Glues and tapes, chosen properly

  • Cyanoacrylate (superglue): hard plastics, ceramic, small rigid repairs. Brittle under impact, useless on flexible joints.
  • Two-part epoxy: metal, glass, ceramic, filling gaps, structural repairs. Mix exactly, and give it the full cure time — usually 24 hours for full strength.
  • Polyurethane wood glue: wood, and it foams into gaps. Clamp it. Stronger than the wood around it.
  • Silicone sealant: waterproofing around baths, sinks and windows. It does not stick to wet or dirty surfaces — clean and dry first, and smooth with a wet finger.
  • Gaffer tape: temporary, strong, leaves residue. Duct tape: general purpose. Self-amalgamating tape: fuses to itself into a waterproof sleeve — the only one that belongs on a leaking pipe.

Doors, windows and draughts

  1. Door that sticks: find the shiny rub marks, then plane or sand only there — a millimetre at a time, testing after each pass. In damp weather a door that sticks will free itself; do not over-correct.
  2. Door that will not latch: tighten the hinges first. Sagging is almost always loose screws, not a worn latch. If the screw holes are stripped, plug them with a golf tee or matchsticks dipped in wood glue, then re-screw.
  3. Draughts: brush strip at the bottom, foam or rubber seal in the frame rebate, and a bead of silicone on the outside where the frame meets the wall. Seal inside and out.
  4. Broken window pane: clear out the glass, remove old putty, bed the new pane in putty, press it in evenly, then putty the outside face and paint it once dry. Tape a plastic sheet over the gap as a stopgap — on the inside, where it stops the wind.
  5. Squeaking hinges: a drop of oil on the pin, not on the leaf. Work the door back and forth a few times.

Roof, gutters and water getting in

  • A ceiling stain means water is finding a path — follow it uphill. Cut a small inspection hole rather than waiting: damp spreads and rots timber.
  • Missing or slipped tiles/shingles: a temporary patch with a plastic sheet tucked under the course above and weighted down will hold for weeks. Fix properly when it is dry.
  • Blocked gutters overflow into the walls. Clear them twice a year, in spring and autumn, and check the downpipes run free by pouring a bucket through.
  • Flat roof blisters or small holes: clean, dry, then a self-adhesive flashing patch, pressed hard. Not a permanent fix, but a dry winter.
  • Never climb onto a roof in wind, rain or frost, and never alone. More people are hurt falling off ladders than by the leak they went up to fix.

Furniture and woodwork

  • Wobbly chair or table: take the joint apart where possible, clean out old glue, re-glue with wood glue and clamp (or bind with tape) for 24 hours. Adding a screw into end grain alone will not hold.
  • Scratch in varnish: rub a walnut or a wax stick along it, then buff. Deeper scratches: fine sandpaper, then a matching wax or oil.
  • Squeaking floorboard: find the joist, drill a pilot hole and drive a screw through the board into it. Sprinkle talcum powder between boards as a quick fix.
  • Water stain on wood: a cloth dampened with vinegar, or an iron on low over a dry cloth, lifts many white rings.

Bicycle, the most useful vehicle in an emergency

  1. Flat tyre: levers out, tube out, find the cause inside the tyre (run your fingers carefully — glass stays embedded), patch or replace, then check the rim tape covers every spoke hole.
  2. Brakes rubbing: check the wheel is seated in the dropouts, then adjust the cable barrel a quarter turn at a time.
  3. Chain skipping: a stretched chain wears the sprockets. Replace the chain early — it is cheap; a new cassette and chainrings are not.
  4. Carry: a pump, tyre levers, a spare tube, a patch kit, a multitool and a chain link. All of it fits in a small saddlebag.

When to stop and call someone

Stop if the job involves: gas appliances or pipework, the inside of a consumer unit, removing a structural wall, asbestos (older artex, pipe lagging, soffits), anything on a roof above one storey, or mains water that will not shut off. These are not skill tests. They are the jobs that injure and kill experienced people.
