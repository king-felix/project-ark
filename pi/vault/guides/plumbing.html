Household plumbing you can fix yourself

Most plumbing emergencies are one of five things: a leak, a blocked drain, a running cistern, a seized valve, or no hot water. All of them can be made safe in minutes with a cloth, a bucket, a spanner and — most importantly — the right shut-off.

Before you touch anything: know your shut-offs. Find the main stop valve (often under the kitchen sink or where the supply enters the house), the valve on the cold feed to the hot-water cylinder, and the stopcock in the street. Turn each one and turn it back now, while the water is still on and the walls are dry. A valve that has not moved in ten years will not move when you need it.

1. A leak you cannot stop

  1. Close the nearest isolation valve, then the main stop valve.
  2. Open the lowest tap in the house to drain the pressure and take the strain off the leak.
  3. Catch the water: bucket, bowl, towels, and a sheet of plastic under it.
  4. Temporary seal: self-amalgamating silicone tape wound tightly and stretched as you wrap, or a rubber patch held with a jubilee clip. Both are meant for a day or two, not a season.
  5. Protect what is below — move furniture, lift carpets, and get air moving afterwards. Damp and mould do more long-term damage than the water itself.

2. A blocked drain or sink

ToolUseCaution
PlungerPlug the overflow, fill the basin enough to cover the cup, then push and pull firmly 10–15 timesWorks on most sink and toilet blocks

Clean a basin trap before anything else: put a bucket underneath, unscrew the two plastic nuts by hand, pull out the U-bend, and clear the sludge. Most slow sinks end there.

3. The toilet will not stop running

  1. Lift the cistern lid. Water above the overflow line means the fill valve is not shutting off; water trickling into the bowl means the flush valve is not sealing.
  2. Turn the isolation valve behind the toilet (or the main) before working.
  3. Fill valve: clean or replace the washer, or swap the whole valve — they cost little and fit with one nut.
  4. Flush valve: clean the seal, check for grit or limescale, and replace the flapper or seal if it is perished.
  5. Adjust the float so the water line sits about 2 cm below the overflow.

4. No hot water

  • Electric cylinder or immersion: check the breaker, then the immersion thermostat (a resettable trip is often a small red button under a cover). Dead immersion elements are replaced, not repaired — and always at the mains off, at both the breaker and the isolator.
  • Gas boiler: check the pressure gauge (typically 1–1.5 bar cold), the thermostat, the timer, and whether the pilot or ignition is working. Do not open the casing. Gas work is not a DIY job — anywhere, ever.
  • Frozen condensate pipe in winter is the most common boiler lockout: pour warm (not boiling) water along the outside pipe, or apply a hot water bottle until it flows.

Smell gas? No switches, no flames, no phones in the room. Open doors and windows, turn the gas off at the meter, get everyone out, and call the gas emergency line from outside.

Basic skills worth practising once

  1. Change a tap washer. Isolate, unscrew the head, remove the old washer, fit the new one the same way up, reassemble, run water slowly and check.
  2. Clear a trap. Bucket under, undo by hand, clean, reseal with a new washer, hand-tight plus a quarter turn.
  3. Isolate and drain a radiator. Useful for leaks and for freezing weather.
  4. Thaw a frozen pipe safely. Warm cloths or a hot-water bottle, working from the tap end back. Never a blowtorch, never boiling water on a plastic pipe.
  5. Wrap a pipe before winter. Foam lagging on every pipe in the loft, garage and outside walls. Cheapest insurance there is.

Preventing the next emergency

  • Every six months: turn every isolation valve fully open and closed, then leave it open.
  • Every winter: lag pipes, and leave the heating on a low frost setting if you go away.
  • Every year: check the flexible hoses on washing machines and under sinks for bulges or rust — replace at five years regardless.
  • Know where the water enters the house, and label the valve with a bright tag.
  • Keep a note of a plumber's number on paper. When the network is down, your contacts list is not.
