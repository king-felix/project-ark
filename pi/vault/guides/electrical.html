Home electrics you can safely touch

There is a short list of electrical jobs a careful person can do: reset a breaker, replace a plug, change a lamp holder, test a socket, run an extension lead sensibly. Everything else — consumer unit internals, new circuits, anything in a bathroom — belongs to an electrician. Electricity is unforgiving, and it does not give second chances.

The non-negotiable rule: isolate, prove dead, then work. Switch off at the breaker, then test with a working voltage tester at the point you are about to touch. A switch that says "off" is not proof. Lock the consumer unit or tape a note on it so nobody restores power while your hands are inside.

What the labels mean

1. A breaker keeps tripping

  1. Unplug everything on that circuit — not just switch off, unplug.
  2. Reset the breaker. If it holds, plug appliances back one at a time until it trips; that appliance is the fault.
  3. If it trips with nothing plugged in, the fault is in the wiring or a fixed appliance. Leave it off and call an electrician.
  4. If an RCD trips rather than an MCB, suspect water ingress, a damaged cable, or an appliance with a heating element. Do not keep resetting it — an RCD that trips repeatedly is doing its job.
  5. Never fit a larger breaker to stop tripping. The breaker protects the cable, and a bigger one lets the cable overheat inside a wall.

2. Replacing a plug (UK 3-pin)

  1. Unplug. Cut the cable cleanly and strip 2 cm of outer sheath without nicking the inner insulation.
  2. Shorten the live (brown, right) and neutral (blue, left) to about 1 cm; leave earth (green/yellow, top, centre) the longest. If the cable is pulled out, the earth is the last to go.
  3. Twist each conductor neatly, fold it, and clamp under the correct terminal screw. No bare copper visible outside the terminal.
  4. The cable clamp must grip the outer sheath, not the inner wires. Tug it to check.
  5. Fit the correct fuse: 3 A for lamps and small electronics, 13 A for heaters, kettles and larger appliances.

3. Lights

  • Lamp not working? Test the bulb in another fitting before assuming a circuit fault. LED bulbs fail more often than the fitting.
  • Whole room out? Check the breaker, then the switch, then the ceiling rose connections — with the circuit isolated.
  • Flickering: almost always a loose connection or a dying LED driver. Loose connections are a fire risk — tighten with the power off.
  • Changing a ceiling rose or lamp holder: photograph the wiring before disconnecting anything. Old houses mix colours; the photograph is your only reliable record.

4. Sockets, extension leads and load

  • A double socket on a ring main can supply 13 A total per plug, but the ring is limited to 32 A overall. Stacking four heaters on one ring will trip it — and heat the cables on the way.
  • Extension leads are rated, usually 13 A fully unwound and often only 5 A when coiled. Never run a heater or a kettle from a coiled reel.
  • Daisy-chaining extension leads is how fires start. One lead, fully unwound, into a wall socket.
  • Warm plugs, brown scorch marks, a buzzing sound or a fishy smell mean stop using that socket now. Replace the accessory, not just the fuse.
  • Outdoors: IP65 rated fittings only, and a plug-in RCD if the circuit is not already protected.

5. Testing without expensive tools

  1. Voltage tester (the two-probe type, with a neon lamp). Test it on a known live circuit — a socket you know works — before and after using it, every time.
  2. Socket tester (plug-in, a few pounds). Tells you at a glance if a socket is wired correctly, has no earth, or has reversed polarity.
  3. Multimeter on continuity: with the circuit dead, checks a fuse, a switch or a length of cable for breaks.
  4. Non-contact pen tester: a rough indicator only. It can miss a live conductor inside a cable; never rely on it as proof of dead.

The full sequence for any job: isolate → lock off → prove dead at the point of work → work → restore → test. Every step matters. Most electrical injuries happen to people who did the first two and skipped the third.

If the power goes out

  1. Check whether the neighbours are out too — one house means your breaker, the street means the network.
  2. Turn off and unplug sensitive electronics (computers, TVs) so the surge when power returns cannot damage them.
  3. Leave one light switched on so you know the moment power returns.
  4. Keep the fridge and freezer shut. Eat what will spoil first.
  5. Do not run a generator indoors, in a garage or next to a window. Carbon monoxide kills quietly. Generators go outside, well away from openings, and are never refuelled while running.
  6. Listen to a battery radio for the official restoration time. Text messages get through when calls do not — and keep your phone above 50 % while the network is up.
